*This proposal comes from an author without formal training in psychology, thus some expected conventions or standards may not be present. The aim is only to suggest one way of testing for a rather broad hypothesis in the behavioral sciences.*

### Introduction

The experiment proposed concerns the choice to take action, and its attribution to a source category. It is based on the assumption that necessity and general desire can be usefully separated even though there may not universally be a strict line. In line with this uncertainty, choice may itself be unconscious, but we digress. The hypothesis of the existence of this separation should face a scenario where groups verifiably differing in internal states are subjected to choices, the consequences of which will be of interest.

### Motivation

The work stems from the urge to better understand the possible origins of choice selection and the respective outcomes to which those origins lead, if at all. It begins with the question of whether one defined origin of choice may produce more undesirable outcomes for the subject than another, and how sensitive the outcomes may be to the origin of choice if the two are indeed related. In ordinary life, we can observe some common actions, or degrees of those actions, having more consequences than others. We can say that the selection may have some source, perhaps one that could be placed on a necessity-optionality scale. This proposal only concerns negative consequences. A connection found between a choice’s origin and its aftermath may help interpret many ordinary actions, or perhaps assist in decision-making.

### Definitions

*Need*: a deficit that is objectively essential to fill in order to maintain one’s wellbeing.<br>*Want*: a desire for some surplus that is not objectively essential for wellbeing. 

We make no claim of awareness or a strict line separating needs and wants. Such things as cravings blur this line enough to resist classification. On the whole, we must still acknowledge that they differ, so that we can discuss choice origins at all in this context.

### Existing Work

The complex emotional responses to indulgence varying with impulsivity have been examined by S. Ramanathan and P. Williams (2007), while the subject of hedonic (want-like) versus utilitarian choice is covered by E. M. Okada (2005). This proposal differs from the former in that the focal subject is the burden of aftermaths across different choice origins, and from the latter in that the negative reactions to those aftermaths are in question, rather than value tradeoffs between hedonic and utilitarian choices.

In economics, negative marginal utility refers to the removed satisfaction resulting from the indulgence in some surplus. Our work is interested in general many-choice scenarios found in ordinary life, to which countable-unit economic terms would not apply.

Our limited search found no experiment directly testing the hypothesis.

### Hypothesis

Actions that stem from want tend to leave behind a more intense negative reaction to their consequences of comparable size than actions that stem from need. The assumption is that it is easier for an actor to be content with the consequences related to need, as such actions may be instantly justifiable. 

### Design

In a buffet setting in the morning, two randomly assigned groups are to have meals of their free choosing. One group has fasted for 12 hours (arbitrary number); the other group has had a filling breakfast 1-3 hours before arrival. Prior to the meal, the groups identify their hunger levels, as well as their emotional states via the taking of pictures and taking a written survey. The pictures are subject to the evaluation of blind raters. Each participant is given equivalent servingware.

After the meal, both groups are subjected to the same survey and picture taking; the results evaluated again by the raters. 

It is optional but encouraged to introduce the task of dishwashing, in order to intensify the reaction to the aftermath and attempt to better reveal the possible irritability. For comparable size, the participants are to wash a fixed amount of dishes, as per their equivalent servingware. Further, this additional task may be known to the groups beforehand, or be a surprise, with potentially different outcomes in each case; that would be outside the simple scenario. 

### Participants 

The participants are to be healthy adults, their recruitment subject to circumstance. A standard calculation provides 80% power in a sample of 64 per group (128 in total), where a medium effect of d = 0.5 and α = .05, two-tailed, can be assumed. Participants who cannot fast for 12 hours, have allergies, do not follow instructions, or learn about the dishwashing task beforehand if that is meant to be a surprise, are to be excluded in advance. Individual sessions should be attempted where possible, but understandably this may not be efficient. Groups of 4 to 6 can be processed, but let us note that participants might influence each other’s emotional states.

### Measures

Irritability can be considered the main interest, but the wider negative-affect PANAS scale of Watson, Clark, and Tellegen (1988) may be more informative, thus it should be the primary measure.
The pictures taken before and after the meal are a secondary measure rated for by the blind raters.
The hunger rating is one question to be asked before the meal: how hungry the participant is, from "not at all" to "extremely." 

### Analysis

The negative-affect from the PANAS-NA scale (Watson et al., 1988) as an irritability proxy is of main concern. It is to be taken from the fasted and fed groups with a t-test of independent samples with a two-tailed α = .05; pre-meal measure as the baseline. Additionally, the blind raters’ scores of eaters’ moods observed from the pictures, before and after the meal, would supplement the respective written tests. 
If the exploration of further, separate measures is of interest, the state of the groups’ servingware can be evaluated after the meal, though that should only be reported as exploratory and used in a different analysis, or for a different hypothesis entirely.

### Limitations

The dishwashing is one way of creating an aftermath, so the result concerns this aftermath and may not carry over to others. 

The contrast between want and need-based action is assumed to be possible to reveal by using and manipulating hunger, as hunger can be referred to as a biological need regardless of circumstances; it is, however, only the author’s assumption that hunger is the optimal selection for the experiment. Hunger itself may affect the irritability proxy in ways that haven’t been considered, such as the hungry group’s potentially higher pre-meal irritability compensating for the post-meal aftermath response. It is also possible that the fed group would refrain from heavily indulging in the first place. Throughout the experiment, the groups are expected to honestly self-report both on hunger and their emotional states. The pictures evaluated by blind raters, though supplementary may be inconclusive. The task of dishwashing is only one attempt to intensify the groups’ emotional reactions to the general aftermath of want-need choices, and may not constitute a fully reliable proxy for aftermaths.
