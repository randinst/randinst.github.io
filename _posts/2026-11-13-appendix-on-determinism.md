The measurement problems on the classical scale and our resulting disregard of some inherent unpredictability originating from chaotic systems invite thought into the notion of determinism in itself. If the strictly practical application of predictive tools is set aside, we are left with a more philosophical question of the resulting description of large-scale phenomena, which would not be too far from the long-standing debate between determinists and indeterminists. With regard to situations where the notion of successive states stops holding, the question becomes whether determinism as a descriptive concept has meaning. The question above arises because one should be able to characterize the behaviour of physical systems or their parts.

Physical laws govern all scales, but despite that, it has been suggested by some that a form of unpredictability may be emerging from interactions between otherwise deterministic physical processes. It is questionable whether cases exist at all in which the object in question depends on the system as a whole rather than a specific area, scale, or period. One would have to use configurations at every scale to account for some claimed unified behavior.

The scale-concerning measurement problem concerns the proposition that if measurements cannot be made (or if they affect outcomes or observations), then definitive claims about indeterminacy or determinism cannot be made. The inability to make predictions does not constitute the system being indeterminate. A system may be unpredictable, or even ‘random,’ in some ways but not others, which can lead us to thinking that nearly every type of ‘disorder’ we define may be disordered in some ways but have order in others.

The claim that natural laws require deterministic evolution is also not to be made. However, if we were to say that measurement determinism needs states, and states cannot exist without a form of time, then determinism in physical reality cannot exist without time. Still, relativity may stop us from using this assumption under strict terms, although it does not annul evolution based on laws. At the speed of light, the next state may become problematic under our assumption because states are tied to time, and there is no photon rest frame and no proper time elapsed along a photon's path. Wave function evolution is also deterministic, as per the lawfulness of the Schrödinger equation, though no wide claim on quantum mechanics should be made just because of it.

Strict determinism likely cannot be attributed to a system at all scales, because under our reasoning, determinism needs a next state which needs a ‘next time’, and both the coupled mechanics and emergent interactions may not allow for unified scales. On the other hand, strict indeterminacy cannot be assumed because of the existence of natural laws which exist under any condition everywhere, and will produce the same outcome in a test with the possible exception in chaotic environments or quantum scales. A case in which determinism or indeterminacy can be applied is one that is isolated enough (no scale-mixing) to prevent couplings. In such a case, the label itself may become a useful reference for anything new introduced to a system. What could be discouraged is placing overarching claims, especially on systems that are chaotic by and large, because those may not only prevent effective practical modelling of those systems but also leak uninformed assumptions onto systems of different varieties.
