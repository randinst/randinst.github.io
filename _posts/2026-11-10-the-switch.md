In a time without calendars, one league off the port of Ponwao stood a tiny island teeming with the rarest plants and reptiles, which was home to a tribe excellently adapted to the equatorial climate and its produce. The natives referred to themselves as Gakwoni, and to the island as Gakwon. Separated from the island by a patch of shallow sea was the habitat of the Pon people, Ponwao. Neither society was known to the other, although the islanders did assume someone's presence beyond the sea passage. 

Strangled by competition from the surrounding land societies and a general tendency to conquer, the Pon were preparing for an expedition to the one island they hadn't been able to reach yet, and thus had no name for it. That island was Gakwon. Of course, there was a reason for it being inaccessible; the sea passage was host to a death trench that began in the shallowest water, which by then had tricked many a crew into attempting to cross a certain kind of light bump. Once the ships had finished balancing on the bump and leaned into the area of the trench, they would get trapped by its walls, their sides getting stuck ever deeper and facing a kind of controlled sinking from which no man could ever return. 

Though they knew of the trench, the Gakwoni were also being forced out of their island, though their reason was quite different. The island they had known for centuries was increasingly rising toward the sky. At first, every month or so, a tribe member would notice that the water below seemed lower at the island's edge. As time passed, the change became noticeable weekly. The rise was accelerating. Leaving soon was a must, or the option of ever leaving would disappear entirely; it was not like the island’s resources had been plentiful since the beginning of time. Of course, the Pon had no idea about this rise.

At roughly the same time, both the Pon and the Gakwon people were selecting their best men and women for their respective expeditions. The only difference was that among the Gakwoni, the majority of the population was to leave in order to establish a camp or two on the mainland, then return for the several remaining members before the island would be too steep to climb into. 

Having prepared their boats and gathered resources, both groups were setting off on their journeys toward one another. 

Only hours into the voyages, both groups were struck by a sudden storm that quickly turned pleasant beginnings into excruciating labours at sea. Each boat was slowly nearing the center of the passage regardless, and in turn, nearing one another. Even prior to reaching the center, even in a rainstorm, the all-conquering Pon had noticed the boat in front of them and begun an ill-founded attempt to fire flaming arrows toward the Gakwoni boat. 

The attack hit the Gakwoni by surprise, and all the tribe had to respond with was a bouquet of spears that couldn't be thrown with any precision or necessary force. Some of the Gakwoni were hit and injured by the arriving arrows, and to one of them the hit was deadly. One flaming arrow had managed to persist, reaching the Gakwoni boat's rear end and causing a small fire with the arrow's oil.

By the time the tribe finished putting out the fire, no new arrows were coming. The Pon boat looked stuck. In just a few moments that followed, the Gakwoni boat itself was hitting shallow ground.

Because of the relatively small size of both boats, the crews were able to go over the bumps without flipping themselves over or getting stuck or dragged down. By then, both were in the area of the trench and paid no attention to one another. They navigated inside the trench, using all strength to go over the bumps on each other's initial sides. After the attempts that seemed as if they’d end it all by flipping both backward and into the depth, the boats went over the shallowest bumps and finally crossed into the other sides.   

The storm was clearing, and past the death trench, there was not one remaining obstacle to face. The Gakwoni reached Ponwao's port, and the Pon people reached the island of Gakwon.

Mainlanders noted to one another how surprisingly "high up" the base of the island turned out to be, but after some rest they began climbing up it. It was somewhat high, but not that high—fifteen yards or less. Having reached the island, they cheered, pitched a tent, and lined up alcohol bottles they had brought. To one Pon's surprise, the figure of a Gakwoni native appeared from behind a stone. The island's guest gasped, jumped, and ran after the native with his knives, catching him and slicing his throat. From the direction of the native's run, a few other figures appeared to the killer. The Pon yelled to his fellow visitors, who eagerly ran with him, carrying their knives, toward the other natives.

In Ponwao, the Gakwoni tribe cheered over its own arrival and started to observe the town surrounding the port. Alert and astonished by the built structures, they hid. They hid around the port for three days, unsure how to travel or how to act in, and toward, a foreign society. Hungry and tortured by exhaustion, and hoping to be welcomed, the Gakwoni decided to try entering the town, but became hunted by their Pon hosts immediately after setting foot in what seemed to be a central market. The tribe escaped through alleys and reached the Ponwao port again. 

Safe from harm, the Gakwoni followed the coast north, disappointed. They raised their heads to look back at the island left behind. In place of their home island, a massive tower-like sea stack stood, staring back at them. The tribe then recalled they had left their children on that now-stack, with just a few handfuls of others to look after them. 

On the cliff of that massive sea stack tower, a man from Ponwao stood, looking down and into the waves that were now crashing hundreds of yards below him.
